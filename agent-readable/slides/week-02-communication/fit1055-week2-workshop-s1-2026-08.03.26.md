## Slide 4: FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2

- FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2
- Speech Act Theory by J.L Austin and John Searle
- Why does this matter for IT professionals?
- Every email, meeting, and presentation is a speech act
- Understanding intent helps you communicate more effectively with colleagues and clients
- Speech acts are the speaker’s utterances that convey meaning and make listeners do specific things (Austin, 1962).
- According to Austin (1962), when saying a performative utterance, a speaker is simultaneously doing something.
- 📖 Key Insight: When we speak, we simultaneously DO something. Every utterance has a purpose.

## Slide 5: FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2

- FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2
- Performative Utterance
- That is why communication is important (verbal or non-verbal (e.g., writing and gestures) because it is the way people express what they need, want, and need to get things done
- Language is central to everything – it makes us uniquely human
- Utterances that are used to perform: requests, warnings, invitations, promises, predictions etcs.
- Example:
- “I am hungry.” expresses hunger, request for something to eat

## Slide 6: FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2

- FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2
- Speech Act in Everyday Utterances
- "You're fired!“
- Expresses both the employment status of the individual in question, as well as the action by which said person's employment is ended.
- "We ask that you extinguish your cigarettes at this time, and bring your tray tables and seatbacks to an upright position."
- Describes the requirements of the current location, such as an airplane, while also issuing the command to stop smoking and to sit up straight.
- "Would it be too much trouble for me to ask you to hand me that wrench?" functions t
- Simultaneously ask two questions. The first is to ask the listener if they are capable of passing the wrench, while the second is an actual request.

## Slide 8: FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2

- FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2
- Common Ground — Herbert H. Clark (1996)
- Goal of communication: achieving mutual understanding between speaker and listener
- 01
- What is Grounding?
- Ensuring both speaker and listener reach shared understanding of what was said and meant.
- 02
- Listener Must...
- Notice · Hear · Understand what was said · Understand what was MEANT
- 03
- Common Ground Accumulates
- Shared history, cultural context, and interaction history all contribute to common ground over time.
- 04
- Why Real Conversation is Messy
- Incomplete sentences, pauses, overlapping turns, unintelligible utterances — all normal!
- 05
- For IT Professionals
- Building common ground with clients and teams reduces misunderstandings and project failures.

## Slide 43: FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2

- FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2
- Stage 2 : Development and Writing
- 4. Develop the introduction, discussion, and conclusion.
- Introduction: Provide a clear introduction to the presentation. Make sure you cover the context, aim, and structure of the presentation. You may say something like this:
- “We will be speaking on the article titled …, which was written by …, who specializes in the field of … as an investigative journalist. [i.e. the context] In this presentation, we will be providing you with the main highlights discussed in the article.., I will be speaking first to provide the introduction, and then X will be discussing the key messages, which will be followed by Y, who will conclude the takeaway message. [i.e. the structure]”
- Conclusion: Make sure you conclude the presentation properly by covering: a summary, a key takeaway point/points, and closing remarks. This may sound like this:
- “Overall, we found that the article provides an interesting insight into the industry, namely… [i.e. a takeaway point] … This concludes our presentation, and thank you very much for listening. Do you have any questions? [i.e. a closing remark]”

## Slide 47: FIT1055: IT Professional Practice and Ethics | Week 2

- Visual Aids & Delivery — Best Practices
- The 10-20-30 Rule (Guy Kawasaki) for presentations:
- 10 slides — the optimal number. More than 10 slides = losing your audience.
- 20 minutes — maximum speaking time to maintain audience attention and energy.
- 30 point font — minimum size. If you need to go smaller, you have too much text on that slide.
